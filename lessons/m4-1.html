The cooking contest with a judge

Imagine a cooking contest. The judge says, "Make me a nice dinner." A weak cook starts chopping onions at once. A strong cook first asks: "How many people? Anyone allergic? Vegetarian? How much time and what budget?" Then she plans the menu, cooks the main dish first, and explains her choices while she works: "I am using less oil because you said light."

The judge is not looking for one correct dinner. There is none. She is watching how you think, ask, decide and explain.

A system design interview is the same contest. You get 45 minutes and a vague sentence like "Design WhatsApp". This lesson shows what the judge wants and how the time usually runs.

What happens in the room (or on the call)

What the interviewer really wants

There is no single right answer. The interviewer watches for four things:

  • Structured thinking: do you break a fuzzy problem into steps, in a sensible order?
  • Trade-offs: do you see that every choice has a cost, and say why you chose one side (CAP, monolith vs microservices)?
  • Communication: can you explain your ideas clearly, and draw them, while thinking aloud?
  • Depth where it matters: can you go deep into one or two parts instead of staying shallow on everything?

They also watch how you handle hints. If they push back, do you listen and adapt, or defend blindly?

A typical 45-minute timeline

The next lesson ( the 7-step framework ) explains each step. Here is the clock:

MinutesWhat you doOutput

How the round differs by company type

TypeWhat to expectHow to adapt
Service companies (large IT services)Often more about basics: three-tier architecture, databases, caching, scaling a simple app. May be combined with a coding round. Sometimes a low-level design question (classes) instead.Be crisp on fundamentals. Explain clearly and avoid fancy buzzwords you cannot back up.
Product companiesA full design round at senior levels, with deep dives, scale and trade-offs. Often a famous system (a feed, chat, a URL shortener, a payment flow).Use the full framework, do the estimates, and be ready for follow-ups like "what if traffic is 10 times?"
StartupsMore practical: "How would you build this with our team of 5?" Cost and speed matter. They may ask about your real projects.Prefer simple and cheap (a monolith, managed services). Show you can ship, and say when you would scale later.

What to do when you are stuck

  • Say so, calmly. "Let me think about this for a moment" is fine. Thirty seconds of quiet is okay; five minutes is not.
  • Go back to what you know. Restate the requirement and ask what a simple version would look like. Start with a single server and a database, and then ask what breaks first.
  • Think aloud. "I'm considering X or Y. X is simpler but Y scales better." Interviewers can only help you if they hear your thinking.
  • Ask for a nudge. "I'm not sure how to handle the hot-shard case. Would you like me to go deeper there, or move on?" Asking is a strength, not a failure.
  • Do not bluff. "I haven't used that, but I'd expect it to work like ..." is better than made-up details.

Trade-offs of the way you spend the time

  • Too long on requirements and estimates leaves no time for deep dives, which is where seniors are judged.
  • Rushing to the diagram risks building the wrong thing.
  • Going deep on everything is impossible. Pick the parts that matter most for this problem and say why.

Keep a clock in your head: by minute 10 you should know what you are building and roughly how big it is. By minute 25 you should have a full picture on the board.

Do it yourself (about 60 minutes, mostly watching)

A. Prepare your notes page

  1. Open a text file or notebook. Make a table with 4 columns: Minute, What the candidate did, What the interviewer said or asked, Your note.
  2. Under it add a second list headed Moments I liked and a third Moments I would do differently.

B. Watch a mock interview and fill the timeline

  1. Open the first YouTube link above. Click the first result that is a full mock interview (a title with "mock" and a design question such as "design Twitter" or "design a rate limiter"). If you do not like it, pick another.
  2. Under the video, check how long it is. Plan to watch the whole thing, with the speed set to normal (click the gear icon on the video, then Playback speed).
  3. Write down the clock time shown on the video at each point the candidate moves to a new stage. Use these stage names: clarify, estimate, API, data, high-level design, deep dive, wrap-up. Pause the video to write (K pauses and resumes).
  4. For each stage, write 1 line on what the candidate said or drew.
  5. Compare: how many minutes did the candidate spend on each stage, against the table above? Which stage was skipped or too long?
  6. Fill the two lists: 3 things the candidate did well (for example "asked about scale before drawing"), and 3 things you would do differently.

C. Your personal timeline

  1. On a fresh page, write the 8 rows of the table in this lesson from memory. Then check against the lesson.
  2. Add a "stuck" card: 3 sentences you will say if you freeze. Example: "Let me take a moment to think." "Let me start with the simplest version and see what breaks." "Which part would you like me to go deeper on?"
  3. Save the file as m4-1 mock-notes.txt in your Module 4 folder.

Quick quiz

What is an interviewer really evaluating in a design round?

Structured thinking, how you reason about trade-offs, communication, and whether you can go deep on the important parts. There is no single correct design.

Roughly how should a 45-minute round be split?

About 2 minutes of intro, 5 on requirements, 3 on estimates, 3 on API, 3 on data, 10 on high-level design, 14 on deep dives, 5 on wrap-up.

Why is jumping straight into drawing a risk?

You may design the wrong thing, because the problem was vague. Questions first show structure and save time later.

What would you do if you do not know how to solve one part?

Say so calmly, state what I do know, start from the simplest version and reason about what breaks, think aloud, and ask whether the interviewer wants me to go deeper or move on. I would not bluff.

How would you change your answer for a 5-person startup versus a large product company?

For the startup, favour simple, cheap choices like a monolith and managed services, and mention when to scale later. For a large product company, show scale thinking, estimates, and deeper trade-offs.

Say it in an interview

"Before I design anything, I'd like to ask a few questions to make sure I solve the right problem."
"I'll spend the first ten minutes on requirements and rough numbers, then draw the high-level design, and then go deep on whichever part you find most interesting."

Common follow-up question: Where do you want to go deeper?

Pick the part that is hardest or most specific to this problem: for a chat app, delivery and ordering of messages; for a feed, how it is built and cached. Say why you picked it, and offer a second option, so the interviewer can steer.

The dabbawala who could tell his own story

Ask two tiffin delivery men about their work. The first says, "I carry boxes." The second says, "I pick up 30 boxes from homes in one area, sort them by colour code at the station, hand them to the next man, and every box reaches the right office by 12:30. Once a train was late, so we split the route in two. Now we keep a spare man for monsoon days."

Both do the same job. Only the second sounds like someone who understands it. He names the problem, the numbers, one hard choice and what went wrong.

Your own projects are the same. "I built a booking app with Next.js" is the first man. This lesson turns it into the second man's story, in about three minutes.

The design story template

Interviewers often start with "Tell me about a project you built." In a design round this is a free chance to show that you think like an engineer. Most candidates waste it by listing technologies. You will instead fill in seven blanks. Each blank maps to something you already learned.

A fully worked example (invented)

This project is made up. The numbers are examples, not real data. Use the shape, not the content.

Project: a tiffin-subscription app.

1. Problem. Home cooks in one city sell monthly meal plans. Customers pause or skip meals by phone call, and the kitchen often cooks too much or too little.

2. Users and scale. About 5,000 subscribers and 40 kitchens. The next day's lunch list is locked at 9:00 pm. About 80% of skip and pause changes arrive in the last hour: 4,000 changes in 3,600 seconds is about 1 a second on average, say 5 a second at the peak. Small, but a spike with a hard deadline.

3. Sketch. Customer app and kitchen app, both calling one API server, which talks to a PostgreSQL database. A scheduled job builds the kitchen list at 9:00 pm. A queue sends the push notifications.

4. Hard decision. Option A: count live, so every kitchen screen runs a query on the changing table. Option B: a batch job freezes a snapshot at 9:00 pm. I chose B. It gave kitchens a stable list and cheap reads. I gave up "last-minute skip at 9:05 pm" and handled it with a small manual exception list. This is a consistency trade-off in simple clothes: a fixed deadline is easier to reason about than fresh data.

5. What broke. The scheduler retried the 9:00 pm job after a timeout, so it ran twice and two copies of the list were sent. Kitchens cooked double. I found it from a log line showing two job ids at the same minute. The fix was an idempotency key per kitchen per date, with a unique constraint in the database, so the second run does nothing.

6. Numbers. Building the list took 40 seconds. After adding an index on (kitchen_id, date) it took 2 seconds. Duplicate lists went from 3 in the first month to 0.

7. At 10x. 50,000 subscribers. First thing to break: the 9:00 pm job and its database reads. I would add a read replica for the kitchen app, split the job per city, and put notifications on a queue with retries and a dead-letter queue.

Do and don't

  • Do pick one hard decision and give both options. "I used Redis" is a tool; "I chose a cache over a faster query because reads outnumbered writes ten to one" is a decision.
  • Do include a failure. Interviewers trust people who admit what broke. A story with no problems sounds invented.
  • Do say "I do not have exact numbers, but roughly..." when unsure. Never invent a precise figure you cannot defend.
  • Don't list every technology. Three minutes is short.
  • Don't claim work that was a team's. Say "we" for the team and "I" for what you did.
  • Don't read from the page. Practice until you can tell it from the seven keywords.

If your project was small, that is fine. Tell the honest scale, then spend more time on blank 7 ("at 10x"). That is where design thinking shows, and it is exactly the skill from the 45-minute round.

Do it yourself (about 45 minutes)

A. Make the template document

  1. Open a new tab with Ctrl + T, type docs.google.com and press Enter. Sign in with your Google account if asked.
  2. Click Blank document (a white page with a plus sign). It may say Blank instead.
  3. Click the title at the top left (it says Untitled document), type Project story 1 and press Enter.
  4. Type these seven headings, each on its own line, pressing Enter after each: 1 Problem, 2 Users and scale, 3 Sketch, 4 Hard decision, 5 What broke, 6 Numbers, 7 At 10x.

B. Fill it in for one of your own projects

  1. Pick the project you know best, even a small one. You should see seven headings with empty space under each.
  2. Under 1 Problem, write two sentences: who needed this and what hurt. Do not write any technology name yet.
  3. Under 2 Users and scale, write the number of users and requests. If you do not know, estimate with the steps in m3-9 and write "about".
  4. Under 3 Sketch, write the boxes in words, for example "browser, Next.js API, Supabase". Then open Excalidraw and draw them in two minutes (a drawing helps, but the words are enough in the doc).
  5. Under 4 Hard decision, write "Option A: ... Option B: ... I chose ... because ... I gave up ...". If you cannot find a decision, look at where you chose a database, a cache, or sync versus background work.
  6. Under 5 What broke, write one real bug, outage or slow page, how you noticed it and how you fixed it.
  7. Under 6 Numbers, write one before and after. If you never measured, open the browser DevTools (F12, Network tab) on a slow page and write what you see now.
  8. Under 7 At 10x, write what would break first and your first two changes. Link each change to a lesson, for example caching or queues.

C. Say it in three minutes

  1. Press the ⊞ Win key, type Clock, press Enter, click Stopwatch.
  2. Shrink your document to just the seven headings (no sentences). Look only at those.
  3. Click ▶ and tell the story out loud, as if to an interviewer.
  4. Stop at the end. If you went over 3:00, cut blank 3 to three boxes and blank 5 to one sentence. If you finished under 2:00, add one number or one trade-off.
  5. Do it three times. You should hear it get smoother each time.

Quick quiz

Name the seven blanks of the design story.

Problem, users and scale, architecture sketch, one hard decision, what broke, numbers, what you would change at 10x.

Why include something that broke?

It shows you have run real systems and learned from them. A story with no failure sounds invented or shallow.

What makes a "hard decision" different from "I used Redis"?

A decision names two real options, what you picked, why, and what you gave up. A tool name alone shows no thinking.

Your project is tiny and has 50 users. What do you do?

Say the honest scale, then spend more time on the "at 10x" blank. It shows you can reason about growth even if you have not lived it.

Which is better: a precise number you are unsure of, or "roughly"?

"Roughly" with a clear method. An invented precise number falls apart on the first follow-up question.

Say it in the interview

"The problem was a hard 9 pm deadline with about 5 changes a second at the peak, so I chose a frozen snapshot over live counting and gave up last-minute edits."
"One thing broke: the scheduled job ran twice after a retry. I fixed it with an idempotency key and a unique constraint."
"At ten times the users, the nightly job and its database reads would break first, so I would add a read replica and move notifications to a queue."

Common follow-up question: "Why did you not choose the other option?" Model answer: "It was better for X, but our users cared more about Y, and the cost of X was Z. If Y changed, I would revisit it."

The water pipe

Imagine a water pipe from a tank to your house. Latency is how long the first drop takes to reach you after you open the tap. Throughput is how much water comes out each second once it is flowing. Bandwidth is how wide the pipe is, the most it could carry.

A long, wide pipe: the first drop is slow to arrive, but then a flood comes. A short, thin pipe: the first drop is quick, but only a trickle follows. They are different things. Fixing one does not fix the other.

Now think of the class results. If 99 students get 80 marks and one gets 0, the average still looks fine. But that one student had a terrible day. Remember this; it is the secret behind percentiles.

Measuring speed the way engineers do

Latency, throughput, bandwidth

WordQuestion it answersUnit
LatencyHow long does one thing take?ms, seconds
ThroughputHow many things get done per second?requests/second, MB/s
BandwidthWhat is the maximum the link can carry?Mbps, Gbps

A 4K video needs bandwidth. A chat message needs low latency. A server that handles 1,000 requests per second has throughput 1,000 rps, even if each request takes 200 ms.

Network speeds are quoted in bits per second (Mbps), file sizes in bytes (MB). One byte is 8 bits, so a 100 Mbps line moves about 12 MB per second at best.

Percentiles: why averages lie

Say 100 requests take these times: 99 take 50 ms and one takes 5,000 ms. The average is about 100 ms. That hides the one awful request. Engineers use percentiles:

  • p50 (median): half of requests are faster than this. The "typical" user.
  • p95: 95 out of 100 are faster than this. 5 in 100 are slower.
  • p99: 99 out of 100 are faster. The slowest 1 in 100.

Why care about p99? A page may make dozens of calls, so most page loads hit at least one slow call. And your heaviest users, who make the most requests, meet the slow tail most often. Goals (called SLOs) are therefore written like "p99 under 300 ms", not "average under 100 ms".

Latency numbers worth knowing

These are rounded, from the well-known "latency numbers every programmer should know" list. They are approximate and vary with hardware; use them for the feeling of scale, not for exact maths.

ActionAbout
Read from memory (RAM)100 nanoseconds
Read from an SSD100 microseconds (0.1 ms)
Round trip inside one data centre0.5 ms
Disk seek (spinning hard disk)10 ms
Round trip India to US150 to 250 ms

The lesson is the ratios. Memory is about 1,000 times faster than SSD, which is far faster than crossing an ocean. So: avoid network trips when you can, keep hot data in memory (caching, a topic coming later), and put servers near the users.

Sizes and powers of two

1 KB (kilobyte)a thousand bytes210 = 1,024

Each step up is about 1,000 times more. Handy facts for quick estimates: a typical short text message or database row is under 1 KB; a web page is around a few MB; a phone photo is a few MB.

Time: a day has 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 seconds, roughly 105. So 1 million requests a day is only about 10 requests per second (1,000,000 / 100,000). Interviewers love this shortcut for converting "per day" into "per second".

Trade-offs

  • Lower latency vs higher throughput. Batching work (sending 100 items together) raises throughput but makes each item wait. Handling each item at once lowers latency but costs more overhead.
  • Caching cuts latency but adds staleness and extra moving parts.
  • Closer servers cut latency but cost more to run in many places.
Do not quote these table values as precise facts. In an interview say "roughly", and use them to compare, for example "memory is orders of magnitude faster than disk".

Do it yourself (about 25 minutes)

A. Ping a near site and a far site

  1. Press ⊞ Win, type cmd, press Enter.
  2. Run a site likely hosted near you. Type it and press Enter (paste with Ctrl + V or right-click): 
    ping google.co.in
    Look at the time= values at the end of each reply line. Write down the lowest and highest.
  3. Now a faraway site. Try a Japanese or Australian one: 
    ping www.yahoo.co.jp
    ping www.abc.net.au
    Write down the times again. Expect larger numbers than the near site, though some sites sit behind a CDN that has a server close to you, so the gap may be smaller than you expect. If a site shows "Request timed out", it blocks ping: try another one.
  4. Compare. The difference is mostly distance and the number of network hops. Check the summary lines at the bottom: Minimum, Maximum, Average. Notice how the maximum differs from the average. That is the idea behind percentiles.
  5. For a longer sample, run ping -n 20 google.co.in (this sends 20 pings instead of 4).

B. Feel latency with throttling

  1. Open Chrome, go to any content-heavy site, press F12, click the Network tab.
  2. Find the dropdown that says No throttling (near the top of the Network panel). Click it and choose Slow 4G. (In some Chrome versions it appears as "Slow 4G" inside a list of presets.)
  3. Reload with Ctrl + R. Wait. The page now loads noticeably slower, and you can see requests queuing in the list.
  4. Look at the bottom bar of the panel. It shows Finish and Load times. Write them down.
  5. Switch back to No throttling, reload, and note the same numbers. The gap is the cost of a slow network.
  6. Leave throttling off when done, or all your browsing will feel slow.

C. Back-of-envelope practice

  1. Pick a number: an app has 5 million requests a day. Divide by 100,000. That is about 50 requests per second on average.
  2. Each request stores 2 KB. Per day: 5,000,000 × 2 KB = 10,000,000 KB = about 10 GB. Write the working in your notes.

Quick quiz

Latency vs throughput: which one is "time for one request" and which is "requests per second"?

Latency is the time for one. Throughput is how many are done per second.

Why is p99 more useful than the average?

The average hides rare slow requests. p99 shows how bad the slowest 1% is, and a page making many calls often runs into those.

Roughly how many seconds are in a day, and how many requests per second is 1 million requests a day?

About 86,400, call it 105. So 1 million a day is about 10 per second.

Roughly rank: memory read, SSD read, India to US round trip, same-datacentre round trip.

Fastest to slowest: memory (~100 ns), SSD (~100 µs), same-datacentre (~0.5 ms), India to US (~150 to 250 ms).

Your users are in India but your server is in the US and pages feel slow. What would you try, and why?

Move the server or add a region closer to users, or put a CDN in front for cacheable content. Each round trip across the ocean costs 150 to 250 ms, and a page needs several.

Say it in an interview

"Latency is how long one request takes; throughput is how many we handle per second. I would track p95 and p99, not just the average."
"Memory is about a thousand times faster than SSD, and a cross-ocean round trip costs hundreds of milliseconds, so I would cache hot data and keep servers close to users."

Common follow-up question: Estimate the load for an app with 10 million requests a day.

A day is about 100,000 seconds, so the average is about 100 requests per second. Peak traffic is usually a few times the average, so I would plan for around 300 to 500 per second, and then size servers and the database for that with some headroom.

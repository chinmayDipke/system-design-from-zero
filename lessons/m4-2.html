The recipe card on the kitchen wall

Even great cooks keep a recipe card. When the kitchen gets hot, they do not want to wonder, "What do I do next?" The card says: wash, chop, heat the oil, add spices, add vegetables, taste, serve. They can spend their brain on the tricky part, the taste.

In a design interview you will be nervous and the question will be fuzzy. A fixed list of steps is your recipe card. You always know what to do next, the interviewer can follow you, and you cannot forget the important parts like numbers and trade-offs.

This course uses a 7-step card. Learn it until it is automatic.

Add them up: 5 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 10 + 14 + 5 = 43 minutes. Add about 2 minutes at the start to listen and repeat the question, and you get the 45 in the round timeline.

Step 1: clarify requirements

Functional requirements are what the system does (users can post, search, pay). Non-functional requirements are how well it does it: speed, scale, reliability, consistency, security. Many candidates forget the second kind, and that is where designs are decided.

Questions about functional scope:

  • Who are the users? What are the 3 to 5 most important things they can do?
  • What is out of scope for today (for example payments, admin screens)?
  • Is it a mobile app, a website, or both? Do we need notifications?

Questions about non-functional needs:

  • How many users, and how many active per day? Which regions?
  • Read-heavy or write-heavy?
  • How fast must it feel (for example, a page in under 200 ms)?
  • Must it always be available, or is a short outage acceptable (availability)?
  • Does it need strong consistency, or is slightly old data fine (CAP, consistency)?
  • How long is data kept? Any privacy or legal rules?

Write the answers on the board as two short lists, then say them back: "So I'll design for ... and leave ... out. Does that sound right?"

Step 2: estimate

Daily users to requests per second, storage per year, bandwidth, using round numbers (estimation). Finish with the design decision it drives ("so we need a CDN and a cache").

Step 3: API design

The handful of calls clients make, for example POST /bookings, GET /lots?lat=..&lng=... Name the inputs and outputs briefly, and mention authentication, pagination and idempotency where they matter (APIs, idempotency).

Step 4: data model

The main entities, their key columns and relationships, and which database fits (SQL or NoSQL) and why (SQL vs NoSQL). Name the indexes your main queries need (indexes).

Step 5: high-level design

Draw the boxes: client, load balancer, app servers, database, cache, queue, storage. Walk one request through it with numbers. Keep it simple first; do not add Kafka until the requirements need it. See drawing a clean diagram.

Step 6: deep dives

Pick the two or three parts where the problem is hardest: double booking, hot keys, ordering, search. Go into detail: algorithms, data layout, failure cases. Let the interviewer steer, but come with your own list.

Step 7: trade-offs, bottlenecks, wrap-up

Name what you gave up ("I chose consistency over availability here"), where the system will break first as it grows, how you would monitor it (observability), and what you would do with more time.

Trade-offs of using a framework

  • It keeps you organised, but do not read it like a script. If the interviewer jumps to step 6 early, follow, and circle back later.
  • Time per step is a guide. Shorten steps 2 to 4 for problems where the deep dive is what matters.
  • Say the step names aloud ("Now I'll sketch the data model"), so the interviewer sees the structure.

Template to paste in your notes: 1 Requirements (must have, not now, scale, speed, consistency, availability) / 2 Numbers (DAU, QPS, peak, storage, cache) / 3 API / 4 Data / 5 Diagram / 6 Deep dives (A, B) / 7 Trade-offs, bottlenecks, next steps.

Do it yourself (about 45 minutes, Excalidraw)

Problem: "Design a parking-lot booking app." People book a parking spot in advance at lots in a city (think of a mall or airport). Set a timer for 40 minutes and go step by step. After each step, compare with the hints, which are only one possible answer.

A. Set up the page

  1. Open excalidraw.com. Press T (text tool), click in the top-left, and type Parking-lot booking.
  2. Make seven empty text areas down the page, labelled 1 Requirements to 7 Wrap-up, with plenty of space. (Or use one Excalidraw page per step.)

B. Step 1: requirements (5 minutes)

  1. Write the questions you would ask (at least 6, using the lists above). Then write the answers you assume:
    • Functional: search lots near a place; see free spots for a time window; book and pay; cancel; the lot operator sees bookings.
    • Out of scope: entry-gate hardware, pricing engine details.
    • Scale: 1 million registered users, 100,000 bookings a day, 10,000 lots.
    • Non-functional: no double booking (strong consistency for bookings); search can be slightly stale; availability 99.9%; booking under 1 second.

C. Step 2: estimate (3 minutes)

  1. Bookings: 100,000 per day / 100,000 seconds = 1 per second average. Peak 3x = 3 per second. Very low.
  2. Searches: assume 20 searches per booking: 2,000,000 per day / 100,000 = 20 per second; peak 60 per second.
  3. Storage: a booking row is about 200 bytes. 100,000 per day x 200 B = 20 MB per day; x 365 = about 7.3 GB per year. Small.
  4. Write the conclusion: "Scale is modest. One relational database with a replica handles it. The hard part is not volume, it is correctness (no double booking)." That is a decision the numbers drive.

D. Step 3: API (3 minutes)

  1. Write 4 endpoints: 
    GET  /lots?lat=..&lng=..&from=..&to=..     -> lots with free spots
POST /bookings   {lot_id, from, to}       -> booking (Idempotency-Key header)
GET  /bookings/{id}
DELETE /bookings/{id}                    -> cancel
  2. Add a note beside POST /bookings: "Idempotency key, so a retry does not book twice" (idempotency).

E. Step 4: data model (3 minutes)

  1. Draw three small boxes (press R) and write the columns:
    • lots: id, name, location (lat, lng), capacity
    • bookings: id, lot_id, user_id, start_time, end_time, status, idempotency_key (unique)
    • users: id, name, phone
  2. Choose the database: PostgreSQL (relational, transactions, constraints; ACID). Note: an index on (lot_id, start_time) for availability checks.

F. Step 5: high-level design (10 minutes)

  1. Draw, left to right: Users (ellipse, O) → Load balancer → App servers (two boxes) → PostgreSQL primary with a read replica. Add a Cache (Redis) next to the app servers for lot details and search results.
  2. Add a Payment provider box on the right, and a Queue to a Notification worker (SMS or email confirmations).
  3. Press A and add arrows. Number them along the booking path: 1 request, 2 load balancer, 3 app server, 4 check availability and write booking in the database, 5 charge payment, 6 put a "booking confirmed" message on the queue, 7 response.

G. Step 6: deep dives (10 minutes)

  1. Deep dive 1: no double booking. Write the problem: two users book the last spot at the same moment. Options: lock the lot's row inside a transaction while counting the overlapping bookings; or keep a per-slot row and use a unique constraint; or an optimistic version check. Pick one, say why (a transaction with a row lock is simple, and at 3 bookings a second it is fast enough).
  2. Deep dive 2: hold the spot during payment. Create a booking as pending with a 5-minute expiry. If payment succeeds, mark it confirmed. A background job releases expired holds.
  3. If time remains, a third: searching lots near a location (a geo index or a bounding box query on lat and lng).

H. Step 7: wrap-up (3 minutes)

  1. Write three lines: Trade-off (strong consistency for bookings, stale search allowed), Bottleneck (the database primary if we reach a very busy day: shard by city), Next (monitoring: booking success rate and payment errors; observability).
  2. Save the file as m4-2 parking-lot.excalidraw. Then read your page top to bottom out loud in 5 minutes, as if the interviewer were there.

Quick quiz

List the seven steps in order.

Clarify requirements, estimate, API design, data model, high-level design, deep dives, trade-offs and wrap-up.

What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?

Functional is what the system does (post, book, search). Non-functional is how well: speed, scale, availability, consistency, security.

Why estimate before drawing?

The numbers decide which problems are real. If scale is small, a simple design is correct; if it is huge, you need caching, a CDN and sharding.

Which steps get the most time, and why?

High-level design (about 10 minutes) and deep dives (about 14 minutes). Deep dives are where depth and judgement show.

The interviewer says "skip the numbers and go to the data model". What do you do?

Follow them. The framework is a guide, not a script. I would state any assumption I need quickly and offer to come back to the numbers if they matter.

Say it in an interview

"I'll follow a structure: requirements, rough numbers, API, data model, a high-level design, then deep dives, and finally trade-offs. Does that work for you?"
"The functional scope is X. For non-functional requirements I'd like to confirm scale, latency, availability and whether we need strong consistency."

Common follow-up question: You have only 20 minutes left and have not finished the design. What do you do?

Say so, and prioritise. Get one complete end-to-end flow on the board first, then pick the single hardest component for a deep dive, and finish with a quick list of trade-offs and what I would build next.
